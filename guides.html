Experimentation Guides

Systematic frameworks for running better experiments, tracking results, and making data-driven decisions about your ML models.

Guide 01 — Tracking

Setting Up Experiment Tracking That Scales: W&B vs MLflow vs Neptune

Experiment tracking is the foundation of systematic ML development. Without it, you are navigating in the dark — unable to reproduce results, compare approaches fairly, or explain why one model outperforms another. The three leading platforms — Weights and Biases, MLflow, and Neptune — take different philosophical approaches to tracking, and the right choice depends on your team size, infrastructure preferences, and workflow requirements.

Weights and Biases provides the most polished experience with minimal setup. A few lines of code instrument your training script, and W&B automatically logs metrics, hyperparameters, system utilization, and even model predictions. Their interactive dashboards enable sophisticated analysis — you can create scatter plots of hyperparameter combinations colored by metric values, compare training curves across dozens of runs, and generate shareable reports that document your experimentation decisions. The managed cloud service means zero infrastructure overhead, though self-hosted options exist for enterprises with data residency requirements.

MLflow is the open-source alternative that integrates well with existing infrastructure. It provides experiment tracking, model packaging (MLflow Models), a model registry for staging and production management, and project packaging for reproducibility. Running MLflow requires managing your own backend (typically a SQL database for metadata and S3/GCS for artifacts), which gives you full control but adds operational complexity. Neptune occupies a middle ground with a managed cloud service that emphasizes collaboration features and supports experiments across ML, LLMs, and data pipelines. Choosing between them requires evaluating your team's needs against their strengths. For a broader view of MLOps tooling, zovo.one provides comprehensive comparisons across the full ML infrastructure landscape.

Key Takeaway

Choose W&B for the fastest setup and best individual/small team experience, MLflow for full control and open-source flexibility, and Neptune for teams that need strong collaboration features. The worst choice is no experiment tracking at all — any of these platforms dramatically improves your ML workflow.

Guide 02 — Hyperparameters

Bayesian Hyperparameter Optimization: Beyond Grid and Random Search

Hyperparameter optimization is one of the most computationally expensive and least understood aspects of ML development. Grid search exhaustively evaluates every combination in a predefined parameter grid, which becomes combinatorially explosive with more than a few hyperparameters. Random search samples randomly from the hyperparameter space and is provably more efficient than grid search for most problems because important hyperparameters often vary along only a few dimensions.

Bayesian optimization improves on random search by building a probabilistic model (typically a Gaussian Process or Tree Parzen Estimator) of the objective function and using it to select the most informative points to evaluate next. This directed exploration means Bayesian optimization typically finds better hyperparameters in fewer trials than random search. Optuna, the leading open-source framework, uses a Tree Parzen Estimator with efficient pruning that automatically terminates unpromising trials early, dramatically reducing compute costs.

The practical workflow involves defining your hyperparameter search space with appropriate distributions (log-uniform for learning rate, categorical for optimizer choice, integer for layer counts), selecting an objective metric, and running the optimization with early stopping. W&B Sweeps and Optuna both support distributed sweeps across multiple machines. A common pattern is to start with a broad random search to identify promising regions, then run a focused Bayesian optimization within those regions. For managing large-scale optimization campaigns, AI workflow tools like those at LockML provide additional infrastructure guidance.

Key Takeaway

Use random search as your baseline, Bayesian optimization when compute is expensive and you have 20+ trial budget, and early stopping always. The learning rate is almost always the most important hyperparameter — optimize it first before tuning others.

Guide 03 — A/B Testing

Model A/B Testing in Production: Getting the Statistics Right

Offline evaluation tells you which model performs better on your test set. A/B testing tells you which model performs better in production with real users. These answers are often different because offline metrics do not fully capture user behavior, latency effects, and the complex interactions between model output and user actions. A/B testing provides the ground truth that offline evaluation approximates.

The most common statistical mistakes in model A/B testing are insufficient sample size (running the test for too few users or too short a time), peeking (checking results before the predetermined sample size is reached, which inflates false positive rates), and ignoring multiple comparison corrections (testing many metrics simultaneously without adjusting significance thresholds). Proper A/B testing requires calculating the required sample size before starting, based on the minimum detectable effect size, desired statistical power (typically 80%), and significance level (typically 5%).

For ML model comparisons, consider the interleaving technique as an alternative to traditional A/B testing. In interleaving, both models contribute to a single ranked result for each user, and credit is assigned based on which model contributed the items the user engaged with. This approach requires 10-100x fewer samples to detect the same effect size compared to traditional A/B testing, making it especially valuable when your traffic is limited. Resources at ClaudFlow offer additional implementation patterns for ML deployment workflows including A/B testing infrastructure.

Key Takeaway

Calculate your required sample size before starting any A/B test. If you cannot afford the traffic for a traditional A/B test, use interleaving for ranking models or shadow deployment for monitoring quality metrics without affecting users.

Guide 04 — Prototyping

Rapid Prototyping for ML: From Idea to Baseline in a Day

The single biggest time waste in ML projects is over-engineering the first version. Teams spend months building data pipelines, training custom models, and optimizing infrastructure before they even know if the problem is solvable or the approach is viable. Rapid prototyping inverts this by getting to a working baseline as fast as possible, then iterating based on what you learn from that baseline's failures.

A good first baseline for text classification is a simple TF-IDF vectorizer with logistic regression — it takes 10 lines of code and runs in seconds. For image classification, a pre-trained ResNet with a replaced final layer takes 20 lines and trains in minutes. For NLP tasks, calling an LLM API with a well-crafted prompt often provides a strong baseline with zero training. These quick baselines establish the performance floor and reveal the characteristics of your problem: is it data-limited? Is the signal weak? Are certain classes harder than others?

Once the baseline is running, conduct ablation studies to understand which components contribute most to performance. Does adding more training data help linearly, or have you hit diminishing returns? Does feature engineering improve performance more than a better model architecture? Does the model fail consistently on certain subgroups or edge cases? These questions guide your next iteration more effectively than guessing at improvements. Document every experiment, even failed ones, because negative results constrain the search space and prevent revisiting dead ends.

Key Takeaway

Build the simplest possible baseline first, measure it thoroughly, and use what you learn to prioritize improvements. One day of rapid prototyping often provides more insight than one month of building an elaborate first version.
